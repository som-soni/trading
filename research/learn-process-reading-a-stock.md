# Reading a stock, top to bottom — one order, every time

**In short**

- Read a stock from the outside in: the market, then its group, then its trend, its base, the entry, the company, and what could gap it. Seven steps, always in that order.
- Each step is a gate. A bad answer early saves you the rest of the work, because no later step can rescue it.
- Evidence will often disagree. Write down which step disagrees and how much it matters, then decide: buy, buy smaller, wait for a trigger, or pass.
- The read ends in a plan, not an opinion: an entry, a stop, a size, and what would prove you wrong.
- A good read can still lose money, and a bad read can still win. Judge the read by whether it followed the steps, not by what the stock did next.

The other guides each explain one piece in depth. This one puts the pieces in order, with two full reads from the past. Each step links to the guide behind it.

---

## 1. Why an order matters

Picture a stock with a beautiful chart. It is in a tight base just under its high, and volume has dried up. It looks like a buy.

Now add one fact: the market fell 6% in two weeks, and only one stock in eight is above its 50-day average. Most breakouts fail in a market like that, however good the chart. The chart was the last thing you should have looked at, not the first.

Reading in a fixed order does three things:

- **It stops early.** If the market says no, you do not need to study the balance sheet today.
- **It stops cherry-picking.** You cannot fall for a chart and then go looking for reasons to agree with it.
- **It makes reads comparable.** Two stocks read the same way can be ranked. A read you can repeat is a read you can improve.

The order runs from what moves the most stocks to what moves only this one:

| Step | Question | Why here |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Market | Are breakouts working right now? | Most stocks follow the market's direction |
| 2. Group | Is money flowing into its industry? | Leaders tend to come in groups |
| 3. Trend | Is the stock itself in an uptrend, and outperforming? | You want a stock already going your way |
| 4. Setup | Is there a proper base, with supply drying up? | The base defines the entry and the stop |
| 5. Entry | Is the price at a low-risk point, with room above? | Where you buy decides your risk |
| 6. Company | Is the business growing, and is the growth real? | Earnings carry the big moves |
| 7. Event risk | What could gap it before a stop can work? | A stop cannot protect you through a gap |

The company comes sixth, not first, on purpose. A swing or position trade is paid by the price moving. A great business in a falling stock is a watchlist name, not a trade. Investors read the same steps in a different order, company first; the steps themselves do not change.

## 2. The seven steps

Each step lists what to look at, what good, mixed and bad look like, and the guide that explains it.

### Step 1 — The market

| Look at | Good | Mixed | Bad |
|---|---|---|---|
| The index against its 50- and 200-day averages | Above both, both rising | Above the 200-day, under the 50-day | Under both |
| Breadth: share of stocks above their 50-day | Over 60% and rising | 40–60% | Under 40%, or falling fast |
| Distribution days in the last 25 sessions | 0–3 | 4–5 | 6 or more |
| New highs against new lows | Highs well ahead | About even | Lows ahead |

A bad market does not mean "sell everything". It means new buys need a much stronger case, and smaller size. See [the index trend](learn-health-trend.md), [breadth](learn-health-breadth.md) and [distribution days](learn-health-distribution.md).

### Step 2 — The group

Ask where its industry ranks against all the others, and which way the rank is moving. A group in the top fifth and rising is a tailwind. A group in the bottom half is a headwind, even for a strong stock.

Then ask where the stock sits *inside* the group. The leader is the one that held up best in the last pullback and made a new high first. See [sectors and groups](learn-sectors-groups.md).

### Step 3 — The stock's trend

| Look at | Good | Bad |
|---|---|---|
| Stage | Stage 2: above a rising 30-week line | Stage 1 basing, Stage 3 topping, Stage 4 falling |
| Averages | Price above the 50-, 150- and 200-day, stacked in that order | Price under a falling 200-day |
| Relative strength | Beating the index over 3 and 6 months | Lagging it |
| Distance from the 52-week high | Within about 25% | More than a third below |

Relative strength is the step most often skipped, and the one that matters most here. A stock that lagged a rising market is telling you something. See [stages](learn-price-stages.md) and [relative strength](learn-price-rs.md).

### Step 4 — The setup

You want a recognisable base: a flat base, a cup, a VCP, an ascending triangle. You also want signs that sellers are running out:

- **Contractions that shrink.** Each pullback inside the base is shallower than the last.
- **Volume drying up** on the right side of the base, below its 50-day average.
- **A base that is not too deep.** Up to about a third in a normal market; deeper bases fail more often.

A stock with no base has no natural place for a stop. That alone can be a pass. See [bases](learn-bases.md), [the VCP](learn-vcp.md), [chart patterns](learn-chart-patterns.md) and [volume](learn-price-volume.md).

### Step 5 — The entry

Now the numbers. Mark three prices:

| Price | Where it comes from |
|---|---|
| **Trigger** | The base's pivot: the high you need it to clear |
| **Stop** | Under the low that would prove the setup wrong |
| **First resistance** | The nearest level above, where sellers waited before |

Then do the arithmetic. The stop should be a few percent away, within your maximum. The room to first resistance should be at least twice the risk. A good stock bought 10% above its pivot is a bad entry. See [support and resistance](learn-support-resistance.md) and [stops and exits](learn-risk-stops-exits.md).

### Step 6 — The company

You are not valuing the business to the rupee or dollar. You are checking that the price move has a reason, and that the reason is real.

| Look at | Good | Warning |
|---|---|---|
| Earnings growth, latest quarter against a year earlier | 20%+ and speeding up | Slowing for two quarters, or negative |
| Revenue growth | Growing too, ideally speeding up | Earnings up while revenue is flat (cost cuts run out) |
| Margins | Steady or rising | Falling |
| Quality | Profits backed by cash, little new debt or new shares | Profits that never turn into cash |
| Valuation | Reasonable against its growth and its own history | Priced for perfection, with growth slowing |

Watch for one-offs. A single tax charge or write-off can make a quarter's growth meaningless and the trailing P/E absurd. See [earnings](learn-fund-earnings.md), [reading results](learn-fund-reading-results.md), [quality](learn-fund-quality.md) and [valuation](learn-fund-valuation.md).

### Step 7 — Event risk

Three questions:

- **When are results?** Within about two weeks, a gap of 5–10% either way is normal. Your stop cannot help through it.
- **How volatile is it?** Compare a normal day's range (ATR) with your stop distance. A stop narrower than one day's range is noise.
- **Is it liquid enough?** You need to get out at your stop, not 3% below it.

If results are close, the usual choices are: wait until after them, buy a smaller size, or pass. See [earnings gaps](learn-strat-earnings-gaps.md).

## 3. When the evidence disagrees

It usually does. The aim is not seven green lights. It is to know which red light you are accepting, and why.

| Conflict | Usual weight | Typical decision |
|---|---|---|
| Great stock, bad market | Market wins most of the time | Watchlist; buy when the market turns, or tiny size |
| Strong market, weak group | Group matters, but a true leader can buck it | Buy only if the stock clearly outperforms; smaller size |
| Great chart, weak earnings | Chart can run, but has no fuel | Shorter trade, tighter management |
| Great company, broken chart | Price wins for a trader | Wait for a new base |
| Everything good, results next week | Gap risk dominates | Wait for the results, or half size |
| Everything good, 8% past the pivot | Entry is bad | Wait for a pullback or a new base |

Write the read down in one line per step, with the conflict named. A week later you can check whether the conflict was the thing that mattered.

## 4. Worked read 1 — HDFC Bank, 2 September 2014 (India)

Prices are split-adjusted, so they are about a quarter of what traded at the time. Everything below uses only what was known on 2 September 2014.

```series
title: HDFC Bank — the read on 2 September 2014, and what followed
from: 2014-03-01
to: 2015-03-31
tf: D
line: india:HDFCBANK.NS | HDFC Bank (split-adjusted) | candles
line: india:HDFCBANK.NS@sma50 | 50-day average
line: india:HDFCBANK.NS@sma200 | 200-day average
line: india:HDFCBANK.NS@vol | Volume | pane2 bars
level: 217.97 | trigger 217.97 | 2014-07-01 2014-11-30 | pivot
level: 210.25 | support 210.25 | 2014-07-01 2014-11-30 | stop
mark: 2014-09-02 | the read
mark: 2014-09-30 | close over the trigger
caption: An ascending triangle under its high, in a market at its own high. The trigger was cleared four weeks after the read.
```

| Step | What the data said | Grade |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Market | Nifty 500 at its 52-week high, 4.5% above its 50-day and 20% above its 200-day. 61% of stocks above their 50-day, up from 48% two weeks earlier. But 5 distribution days in 25 sessions | Good, one warning |
| 2. Group | Private banks ranked in the bottom third of groups | Bad |
| 3. Trend | Stage 2. Close 214.80, above a rising 50-day (206.97) and 200-day (184.72), 0.2% under its 52-week high. But it lagged the index by 4.8 points over three months | Good trend, weak relative strength |
| 4. Setup | An ascending triangle, 29% deep over the whole base, with shrinking pullbacks. Volume had not dried up: the last ten days ran above average | Mixed |
| 5. Entry | Trigger 217.97, 1.5% above the close. Support at 210.25 gave a stop about 3.5% under the trigger. No resistance above: it was at a high | Good |
| 6. Company | Earnings up 19.5% on a year earlier, slowing from 26% three quarters before. Net margin steady near 17%. Trailing P/E about 23 | Good, slowing a little |
| 7. Event risk | Next results about seven weeks away. A normal day's range was 1.7% | Good |

**The conflicts.** Two red lights: a weak group, and a stock that had lagged a strong market. Against them: a strong market, a clean pattern at a high, and steady earnings.

**A reasonable decision.** Do not buy on 2 September. Set an order to buy above the trigger, at reduced size because of the weak group and relative strength. Stop under support.

**What happened.** The stock closed above 217.97 on 30 September, at 218.16. It never went back to the stop. By 4 March 2015 it touched 277.33, 27% above the trigger.

The read was not perfect, and the reduced size cost some of that gain. That is the price of respecting a red light. Over many reads, the red lights are right more often than this one suggests.

## 5. Worked read 2 — Microsoft, 12 October 2018 (US)

```series

from: 2018-06-01
to: 2019-05-31
tf: D
rebase: 100
line: us:MSFT | Microsoft
line: us:SPY | S&P 500 ETF
mark: 2018-10-12 | the read
mark: 2018-12-26 | low
mark: 2019-03-18 | new high
caption: Rebased to 100 on 1 June 2018. Microsoft held up better than the market, but still fell 14% after the read.
```

| Step | What the data said | Grade |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Market | S&P 500 4% under its 50-day and at its 200-day, 6% off its high. Only 12% of stocks above their 50-day, down from 54% two weeks earlier. 6 distribution days in 25 sessions | Bad |
| 2. Group | Infrastructure software still ranked well, but falling; none of its members above their 50-day | Weakening |
| 3. Trend | Stage 2, above a rising 200-day (99.49), just under the 50-day (110.68). 5.7 points ahead of the index over three months. 6% under its 52-week high | Good |
| 4. Setup | A long, contracting base, 30% deep at most. No pattern complete yet. Volume heavy: the sell-off was being sold | Mixed |
| 5. Entry | No trigger yet: the base's high was 116.18, 6% above. Support near 105–107 | Not yet |
| 6. Company | Revenue up 17.5%, faster each quarter for a year (11.9% a year earlier). Net margin 29.5%. Trailing P/E showed 52, but that was distorted: a one-off tax charge had wiped out a quarter's earnings | Good, once the one-off is set aside |
| 7. Event risk | Results 12 days away. A normal day's range was 2.4% | Bad |

**The conflicts.** An excellent company and a strong stock, against a bad market, heavy selling and results inside two weeks.

**A reasonable decision.** Pass for now. Keep it at the top of the watchlist, with an alert above 116.18 for when the market recovers.

**What happened.** Microsoft fell to 93.96 on 26 December, 14% under the price on the day of the read. Then the market turned. Microsoft closed above 116.18 on 18 March 2019, at 117.57, and reached 121.58 by mid-April.

The read was right twice. It said "not now" before a 14% fall. It also kept the stock on the list, so the buy came when the market and the base were both ready.

## 6. Common mistakes

- **Starting with the chart or the story.** Once you like a stock, every later step looks better than it is. Start with the market.
- **Treating a mixed read as a yes.** "Mostly good" with a bad market is a no for most traders, most of the time.
- **Skipping the arithmetic.** A read without a stop and a size is an opinion. Step 5 turns it into a trade.
- **Reading the company with today's numbers.** For a past date, use only the quarters already reported then.
- **Ignoring one-offs.** A tax charge, a write-off or a big asset sale can turn growth and P/E into nonsense for a year.
- **Judging a read by its result.** Read 1 above made money with two red lights; that does not make red lights safe.

## 7. What the evidence says

No study tests "the seven steps" as a whole. Each step rests on research covered in its own guide:

| Step | Evidence, in short | Guide |
|---|---|---|
| Market | Stocks move together; market timing by trend has a long record, weaker recently | [the index trend](learn-health-trend.md) |
| Group | Industry momentum explains much of stock momentum (Moskowitz and Grinblatt, 1999) | [sectors and groups](learn-sectors-groups.md) |
| Trend and relative strength | Momentum: past winners keep winning for 3–12 months (Jegadeesh and Titman, 1993) | [relative strength](learn-price-rs.md) |
| Near the high | Stocks near their 52-week high do better (George and Hwang, 2004) | [relative strength](learn-price-rs.md) |
| Earnings | Prices drift after earnings surprises (Bernard and Thomas, 1989) | [earnings](learn-fund-earnings.md) |
| Quality | Profitable, cash-backed firms do better (Novy-Marx, 2013; Sloan, 1996) | [quality](learn-fund-quality.md) |

The order itself is a practical choice, not a finding. It puts the broadest, most-tested effects first.

## 8. Checklist

Write one line for each step, with a grade: good, mixed or bad.

1. **Market:** index against its averages; breadth; distribution days.
2. **Group:** its rank, and which way the rank is moving; the stock's place in the group.
3. **Trend:** stage; the averages; relative strength over 3 and 6 months; distance from the high.
4. **Setup:** the base's name, depth and length; shrinking pullbacks; volume drying up.
5. **Entry:** trigger, stop and first resistance; risk in percent; room in R.
6. **Company:** earnings and revenue growth, and their direction; margins; cash; one-offs; valuation.
7. **Event risk:** days to results; a day's range against the stop; liquidity.
8. **Conflicts:** which steps disagree, and how much each matters.
9. **Decision:** buy, buy smaller, wait for a trigger, or pass. Then entry, stop, size and what proves you wrong.
