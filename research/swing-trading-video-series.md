# Swing trading — notes on "The Long & The Short", episodes 42–44

*A three-part video series by Sandeep Rao: "What Is Swing Trading (And Why Most Beginners Get It Wrong)" (Ep. 42),
"The Easiest Way to Find Swing Trading Stocks" (Ep. 43) and "Build a Complete Swing Trading System" (Ep. 44),
about 54 minutes in all. These notes were made from transcripts of the videos (October 2026). They record what
the series says, in its order, then map each idea to where it lives in this app and what our own data says about
it. The series is the source for the ideas; nothing here endorses its parameters.*

**In short**

- Swing trading has no agreed definition. The working one: hold for days to a few weeks to capture **one
  identifiable move** in either direction, in any instrument.
- There are many ways to identify a swing. The series lists seven: percentage swing charts, three-day swing charts,
  Taylor's three-day cycle, flags and pennants, N-day breakouts, trend alignment across timeframes, and the
  strongest stock in the strongest group. The presenter prefers the last two.
- For stocks, he picks the **strongest stock in the strongest sector**, judged on **equal-weight** sector baskets,
  enters on a trend signal (Supertrend, Renko, an N-day breakout or a flag), and **trails** instead of targeting.
- The system: a **category trend filter** (the stock's own segment index above its 21-day average), **five
  positions** of 20% each, **one in, one out**, refilled from a scan of the week's best performers, one consistent
  trailing exit, risk-based or equal sizing, and a reward-to-risk of at least 3 to 1.
- The same system can run on a **basket of liquid ETFs** — gold, silver, index ETFs — for a smoother equity curve.

## Episode 42 — What swing trading is

### The opening story

The presenter met swing trading in 2002 through a book a friend wanted to share the cost of: "buy a stock, hold it
a few weeks, make 5–10%". With a net worth of about ₹10,000 and no demat account, he opened one, traded, and stopped
after losing ₹6,000. The series is what he would do differently now, in three parts: the concept, discretionary
stock selection, and a systematic version with position sizing and risk management.

### What "swing" means, and where the word comes from

- **No dictionary definition.** Marc Rivalland (*Marc Rivalland on Swing Trading*) says up front that, unlike day
  trading or long-term investing, swing trading is not a well-defined term. His working definition: a trade that
  seeks to capitalise on short-term upswings and downswings in share prices.
- **Charles Dow** (Wall Street Journal editorials, around 1900) described a *medium swing* of 10 days to 3 months
  and a *short swing* of hours to a month. "Swing" then described how long a move lasts, not a trading style.
- **Linda Bradford Raschke** traces the technical sense back about a hundred years, to Dow and W.D. Gann: trade
  one length of the swing at a time. The term was also used for in-one-day, out-the-next trades.
- **Jesse Livermore**, through Edwin Lefèvre's *Reminiscences of a Stock Operator*, uses "swing" for a whole
  primary trend — and argues *against* trading short swings: sit through the whole move. The man most associated
  with the word was closer to a trend holder than a swing trader in today's sense.
- **Richard Wyckoff's** correspondence course (1931) describes small daily waves building into larger waves and
  then into bull or bear market swings: a wave inside a wave inside a wave. Trading the middle wave is the essence
  of a swing.
- **Bottom line:** trades held for days to a few weeks, capturing one identifiable trending move, in either
  direction, across any instrument.

### Seven ways to identify a swing

1. **The percentage swing chart (Perry Kaufman).** Kaufman calls it the earliest of all systems. A new high above
   the previous swing high is a buy; a new low below the previous swing low is a sell. It is like point and figure
   without a box size, and it is **time-agnostic**: a swing counts only once price has moved a minimum threshold,
   say 5%, from the last extreme. A stock flat for two months still signals the moment it breaks out.
2. **The three-day swing chart (Gann, as Rivalland uses it).** Gann called it his mechanical method and trend
   indicator. As prices rise you connect the daily highs; the line turns down only after **three consecutive down
   days**, then connects the lower lows, and the reverse on the way up. Rivalland modifies the three-day rule to
   respond faster. A swing is defined by counting days of reversal, not by percentage.
3. **Taylor's three-day method** (George Taylor, *The Taylor Trading Technique*, 1950): a buying day, a selling day
   and a short-sale day, cycling in turn. Markets move in a repeating rhythm of roughly three phases; swing trading
   is the same action on an enlarged scale.
4. **Flags and pennants.** Discretionary and hard to define with rules, but easy for a trained eye. A sharp,
   near-vertical move (the flagpole), then a brief pause: a flag drifting gently against the trend in a tight
   parallel channel, or a pennant coiling into a small triangle. Entry is the breakout in the direction of the pole.
   Described by Richard Schabacker and by Edwards and Magee.
5. **The N-day breakout (Kaufman).** Instead of crossing the last swing high, ask whether price crossed the highest
   high or lowest low of the past N days. Kaufman tested it against his swing chart on SPY, QQQ and Amazon: both made
   roughly similar money, by very different routes and win rates, depending on the stock and the volatility regime.
6. **Trend alignment across timeframes (Deron Wagner).** Basic trend filter: the 50-day average above the 200-day,
   both sloping up. The discipline is checking that alignment on the **daily, weekly and monthly** charts before
   entering. "We are looking for a stock that's ready to take off on the daily, weekly and monthly timeframes." A
   strong monthly uptrend with a daily downtrend is not the best scenario — you want convergence, not conflict.
7. **The strongest stock in the strongest group (Wagner).** Start one level up: which sector, sub-sector or market-cap
   category is leading? New traders chase the best-looking chart even in a weak group; Wagner would rather buy "the
   second or third best-looking chart in a strong industry group than the best-looking chart in a weak one".

**Left off the list on purpose: O'Neil's cup with handle.** It blends a chart pattern with fundamental filters
(earnings growth, institutional ownership), and telling a proper cup from a failed one needs a trained eye more than
a formula — harder to quantify than a percentage swing chart or an N-day breakout.

The presenter finds methods **6 and 7** the most convincing.

### Instruments and timeframes

- **Stocks** — the stock itself, or its futures and options for leverage, liquidity permitting.
- **Indices** — ETFs, or index futures and options.
- **Commodities** — gold, crude oil, aluminium; in India only as futures or options, no spot equivalent to buy and hold.
- **Timeframe:** daily bars as the finest granularity. Daily, weekly and monthly data is clean and easy to get.

### Relative against absolute momentum

- For stocks you can compare an instrument with its peers — IT against IT, midcaps against midcaps. That is
  **relative (cross-sectional) momentum**: is it stronger than its group?
- Gold, crude, aluminium or the Nifty itself have no group. For them you need **absolute (time-series) momentum**:
  is the instrument stronger than its own past — than a month, three months or six months ago — whatever else does?
- So the toolkit depends on the instrument: stocks allow best-stock-in-best-group; standalone instruments only allow
  their own trend.

## Episode 43 — Finding stocks, with judgement

### Discretionary still means rules

- Discretionary trading leans on judgement, pattern recognition and experience; systematic trading on fixed,
  testable rules. Neither is superior; they suit different temperaments and time budgets. Swing trading is an area
  where both work. (The presenter's earlier Ep. 3 covers the trade-off.)
- He has never seen a successful discretionary trader without rules for why they enter, where they exit and, if
  they use one, the target. **Discretion is in applying the rules — the selection — not in having none.**
- Alan Farley (*The Master Swing Trader*): a valid discretionary approach "requires a heavy dose of personal
  responsibility" — there is no one else to blame.

### Why the long side, for Indian equities

1. **Few stocks can be shorted.** A liquidity floor might leave about 500 tradable stocks; only about 200 have
   derivatives, and only some 50–70 of those are genuinely liquid in F&O. Swing shorts are confined to that pool.
2. **Equities drift upward.** Flows can lift an index or a stock at any time; markets spend far more time rising than
   falling. On opportunity alone, the long side is better.
- The upward drift is an equity phenomenon. Commodities do not have it, so for them both sides are considered.

### Strongest stock in the strongest sector — the method

- Once you know where the money is flowing by sector or industry, finding the winning stock is far easier.
- **Two ways to find leading sectors in India:**
  1. **NSE sectoral indices** — NSE publishes 34, about 17 actively followed. Track those, or build your own
     equal-weight trackers for the other 17.
  2. **Granular trackers like the Tijori indices** — about 80 niche sub-sectors that expose momentum in pockets most
     traders miss. They include small and micro caps, so the playground expands — but most of those stocks are
     outside F&O, so they are cash, unleveraged trades only.
- **The problem with NSE's sector indices: they are cap-weighted.** One or two heavyweights skew the whole index.
  For swing trading you need an **equal-weighted** view to see whether most of the basket is taking part.
  Building your own equal-weight trackers is, in his view, the only long-term solution.

### Building an equal-weight basket (worked example)

- Download a sector's constituents from niftyindices.com (Indices → Sectoral indices → e.g. Nifty Realty → index
  constituents), then enter them into a TradingView script that plots an **equal-weight NAV** of the basket from a
  chosen start date (he uses 1 January 2026), with 20-, 50- and 200-day EMAs on it, a table of 1-day, 1-week,
  1-month, 3-month and 6-month returns, and a **stock-against-NAV** table: each member's return since the start
  date, and how far ahead of or behind the basket it is.
- **Nifty Realty** example: the basket was in an uptrend (the EMAs had crossed a few days earlier) and positive over
  three months, less so over six. Since 1 January the basket was up only 1.66%, but four stocks had done far better
  than the rest — a rally carried by a few stocks, not a broad sector trend.
- Pick a strong member (Oberoi Realty in the example), apply a trend indicator — his favourite is **Supertrend with
  the default (10, 3)** on daily bars — and check it is in an uptrend. Scan sectors and stocks daily or weekly.
- **Renko charts** as a second view: price-only bricks that ignore time, so long sideways stretches compress and
  all-time-high breakouts stand out. In the example one stock was still below its December 2024 high, while
  **Phoenix Mills** had broken its July 2024 all-time high — "there could be a story brewing".
- **Tijori example:** set the Tijori indices to equal weight, sort by one-week return and check one-month and
  three-month returns. Top was **microfinance** (about seven stocks); rebuilt as an equal-weight basket it was up
  17.6% since 1 January, again led by three stocks. **Fusion** had a Supertrend uptrend from late June, but Renko
  showed it far below its 2023 all-time high (about 650 against 200–250 now).
- The indicators give an entry and a trailing method, which is also the stop.

### Trail, don't target

- He is a proponent of **trailing rather than exiting at targets**, because it is very hard to predict when a trend
  stops. Some of your best trades may run for six months — why exit and reallocate?
- Trail with moving averages or Supertrend.

### Diversify

- Concentration looks good when you are right; **diversification keeps you alive**. Position sizing matters more to a
  trader's longevity than stock selection, which is the easy part because rules are easy to build around it.

## Episode 44 — A complete, systematic swing system

### Be in sync with the market: the category filter

- "Be in sync with the market" means checking what the stock's own universe is doing. A **midcap** stock: look at the
  **Midcap 150**. Small or micro caps: their own indices.
- Put a **trend filter on that base index**. On daily bars, a **21-day or 50-day moving average**: if the index is
  below it, do not swing-trade any stock belonging to it, because the odds of trends forming are low. A rising tide
  lifts all boats.

### Three entry methods, each gated by the category filter

1. **Renko** (in place of Kaufman's swing chart; it serves the same purpose of filtering noise). Brick size **5%**: a
   green brick prints only after a full 5% rise from the last brick; a red one only after a 5% fall. Wait for one
   green brick — ideally **two** — to confirm the turn, and take it only if the stock's sector or cap index is above
   its 21-day average.
2. **N-day breakout.** Look-back of **7, 15 or 21 days** (his example uses 10): a close above the highest high of the
   period is a bullish breakout, below the lowest low a bearish one. The breakout is only the trigger; the category
   must be aligned.
3. **Flags and pennants**, detected automatically by a built-in TradingView indicator (with adjustable definitions).
   A close above the upper boundary is a long entry, below the lower a short. Again only with the category aligned.

Parameters are a structure, "not rules carved in stone".

### The portfolio

- A hard cap of **five concurrent positions** (scalable to ten); five means **20% of capital per trade**.
- Start: run the scan with fixed metrics and allocate to all that qualify, up to five.
- **One in, one out:** when the portfolio is full, no new trades. When a position hits its exit and frees capital,
  run the scan again and fill the slot with the next best candidate.
- Each position has its own **trailing stop** — no fixed targets, no fixed holding periods.

### The scan

- TradingView's scanner with a simple **percentage-change rule**: the **top performers over the past week**, ranked by
  relative strength, form the core basket.
- Then either inspect each for a technical pattern, or take the top ones as they are.
- To tighten it: price **above the 200-day or 50-day** average, and a **minimum average volume**.
- Discretion at the final step is optional; run it blind as a pure rules system if you prefer — both work.

### Three ways to trail

1. **A moving average** on daily bars: the **21-day** for breathing room, or **9- or 7-day** to trail closely. He
   prefers **EMAs**, which react faster.
2. **Supertrend**, which adjusts for volatility through the ATR better than a plain moving average.
3. **A fixed percentage from the highest price** reached — e.g. a **3–4%** buffer below the peak; out when price falls
   that far from its high.
- The golden rule: **pick one that fits your risk tolerance and stick to it** — do not switch indicators mid-trade.

### Position sizing

- **Equal allocation:** five positions, 20% each.
- **Risk-based:** every setup has a predefined exit, so you know the distance to the stop before entry; work
  backwards from the money you will risk. Example: a ₹10 lakh portfolio risking 1% (₹10,000); buy at ₹500 with the
  stop at ₹480 → ₹20 a share → **500 shares**. If the stop is hit, the loss is ₹10,000 whatever the share price.
- **Capital discipline:** never exceed the capital pool dedicated to the system (not ₹5 lakh one week and ₹7 lakh the
  next). Scale up gradually as the account grows.
- **Reward to risk of at least 1 : 3.** Losing streaks and clusters of stop-outs are inevitable; a high reward-to-risk
  means a handful of winners offsets many losses. Trailing stops help: some trades give far more than 3 : 1.

### Swing-trading ETFs instead of stocks

- Less risky: no single-company overnight risk or severe gap downs.
- Opens other asset classes — **gold, silver, US index ETFs**.
- The system is unchanged: same entries, trailing exits, scan and allocation, applied to a basket of ETFs.
- Caution: only **liquid** ETFs with real volume; many ETFs exist in name only.

## Where our evidence disagrees with the series' settings

- **The one-week top-performer scan.** Published research finds returns over one week to one month tend to
  **reverse** (the short-term reversal effect); momentum works over 3–12 months. Ranking on last week's gain leans on
  the wrong side of that. The guides *Relative strength* and *Cross-sectional momentum* cover the research.
- **A 3–4% trailing stop.** *Stops and exits* measured trailing exits on stored data: tight trailing stops did poorly,
  selling good trades on ordinary pullbacks, and wider trend exits did better. A 3–4% trail is tighter than three
  ATRs for most stocks. `--exit-mode trail_pct` exists so it can be measured rather than argued.
- **Kaufman's comparison** (swing chart vs N-day breakout making similar money by different routes) and the other
  methods are claims of the series' sources; they have not been reproduced here except where a guide says so.
