# Support and resistance — drawing the levels that matter

**In short**

- Support is a price area below the market where buyers have stepped in before. Resistance is an area above it where sellers have. Draw them as zones, not exact lines.
- Start on the weekly chart and work down. A level must be earned: price turned there clearly, ideally more than once and from both sides.
- Draw each zone from where the candle bodies cluster to where the wicks reach. Ignore a lone spike, and keep the zone no wider than a typical bar's range.
- Grade every level and keep only a handful near price. A broken level is kept and relabelled, because old resistance often becomes support.
- Levels mark where to pay attention, not what will happen. Measured on stored prices, zones drawn by fixed rules held no more often than chance; only levels that had flipped roles did a little better.

This guide is about drawing horizontal levels: which turning points count, how wide a zone is, how to grade it, and what to do in common situations. Why levels exist, and the demand and supply zones a strong move starts from, are in [Supply and demand](learn-supply-demand.md).

## 1. A level, and why it works

In June and July 2025, ICICI Bank dipped to about ₹1,404 and then ₹1,408, and buyers stepped in both times. In late September it closed below ₹1,393, and the floor was gone. Over the next seven months, five rallies stopped between ₹1,393 and ₹1,445.

The old floor had become a ceiling. The stock finally closed above it in July 2026, but fell back below in early September.

```series

from: 2025-04-01
to: 2026-10-15
tf: D
line: india:ICICIBANK.NS | ICICI Bank
line: india:ICICIBANK.NS@sma200 | 200-day average
mark: 2025-07-18 | buyers step in near ₹1,408
mark: 2025-09-24 | closes below the zone
mark: 2025-10-24 | rally stops at ₹1,445
mark: 2026-01-16 | and again at ₹1,444
mark: 2026-04-24 | and at ₹1,393
mark: 2026-07-20 | closes above at last
mark: 2026-09-09 | falls back below
caption: The same band, about ₹1,393 to ₹1,445, stopped declines in 2025 and rallies in 2026.
```

Notice that no two turns happened at the same price. They landed somewhere inside a band about 4% wide. That band is the level.

Levels work for three reasons:

- **Memory.** Traders remember prices where they made or lost money. Many act when price returns there: a buyer who is underwater sells at breakeven.
- **Order clustering.** Large orders, stops and targets gather at obvious prices: earlier highs and lows, and round numbers.
- **Self-fulfilment.** So many people watch the same areas that their orders partly create the reaction they expect.

An honest note: there is no single rulebook that every good trader agrees on. What follows are the principles that recur across respected price-action teaching. Where practitioners disagree, the guide says so.

## 2. Five principles

1. **Levels are zones, not exact lines.** Price overshoots a little or falls short a little. A line is fine as shorthand, but expect the reaction somewhere in a band.
2. **Higher timeframes carry more weight.** A weekly level reflects months of decisions; a level on an hourly chart, a few days. When they conflict, the higher one usually wins.
3. **A level must be earned by a clear reaction.** Price should have turned there and moved away sharply. A spot where price only drifted sideways is weak.
4. **Broken levels often flip roles.** Old resistance, once broken, frequently becomes support, and the reverse. This is role reversal, also called polarity.
5. **Fewer, cleaner levels beat many.** If every price is near some line, the lines say nothing. Keep the few that matter and are near enough to price to be relevant.

## 3. Drawing step by step

The order matters: each step narrows the next.

1. **Start from a clean chart.** Remove indicators and old drawings. The levels should come from what price did, not from an indicator's opinion.
2. **Pick two or three timeframes** that suit how long you hold a trade. A swing trader might use weekly and daily charts.
3. **Begin on the highest one, zoomed out.** Show enough history for several major swings. If there is an all-time high or low, keep it in view.
4. **Mark the obvious turning points first.** Step back from the screen. The swing highs and lows that jump out are the ones everyone else sees too.
5. **Look across for areas price turned at more than once,** ideally from both directions. An area that was support at one time and resistance at another is especially strong.
6. **Draw each as a zone** covering the cluster of turns (section 4 explains the edges).
7. **Grade each one and keep the strong ones** (section 5). On the highest timeframe that usually leaves only a few near price.
8. **Move down a timeframe.** Keep the higher zones, drawn heavier. Add only levels that fall in the gaps between them, or that sharpen an existing zone.
9. **Check for confluence** with round numbers and the 50- and 200-day averages, which strengthen a level.
10. **Ask whether another trader would draw roughly the same zones.** If not, you are probably forcing levels that are not there.

## 4. Wicks, bodies and how wide a zone is

This is where traders disagree most. The wick camp says the wick shows the true extreme, where orders were filled. The body camp says closing prices show where the market settled, and wicks are often brief spikes or stop runs.

The usual compromise uses both:

- **One edge goes where the candle bodies cluster.** For support, that is where the bottoms of the bodies bunch together. For resistance, the tops.
- **The other edge goes where the wicks reach.** For support, the lower wicks; for resistance, the upper ones.
- **Ignore a single wick that sticks out alone.** It was probably a spike, and including it makes the zone too wide to use.

As a guide to width, keep a zone no wider than about one typical bar's range on its timeframe. The average true range (ATR) measures that range. If a zone must be wider to cover every turn, it may really be two levels, or a weak area that deserves no zone at all.

When you need a single line, for an alert, use the edge price will reach first. For support that is the top edge; for resistance, the bottom.

## 5. Grading a level

Not every level deserves the same trust. Score one point for each factor a level meets. The factors are listed from most to least important.

| Factor | What makes the level stronger | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Timeframe | Found on the weekly chart, not only the daily | More money and more people decided there |
| Rejection | Price left quickly, with large bars | A sharp move shows a large imbalance of orders |
| Role reversal | Acted as both support and resistance | Buyers and sellers both have history there |
| Touches | Two or three clean reactions | More traders have noticed it |
| Volume | Heavy volume on the reaction | Real participation, not a thin drift |
| Confluence | A round number or a widely watched average sits inside | Several groups of traders watch the same area |
| Recency | A reaction within the recent history | Today's traders still remember it, and may hold positions there |

Treat a level that scores well on several factors as strong, and one that scores on only one or two as weak.

**The touches debate.** More touches make a level better known, but each test also uses up orders waiting there. After several tests a level often weakens rather than strengthens. Repeated tests in quick succession, each bounce smaller, are a classic sign of a coming breakout. ICICI Bank's zone above was tested five times from below before price closed through it.

## 6. Kinds of levels

The first four are accepted by nearly all price-action traders. The rest are popular, but many treat them only as confirmation.

| Kind | How it is drawn | Trust |
|---|---|---|
| Horizontal swing levels | Two or more swing highs or lows at about the same price, covered by a zone | The foundation: draw these first |
| Previous period highs and lows | The last week's, month's and year's high and low, as single lines | Widely watched |
| Role-reversal levels | A broken level kept and relabelled | The first retest after the break is usually the most reliable |
| Round numbers | Prices ending in 00 or 50, such as ₹1,000 or $100 | Strongest when they line up with a swing level |
| Trendlines and channels | At least two higher lows (or lower highs), confirmed by a third touch | More subjective: two traders draw them differently |
| Moving averages | The 50- and 200-day averages | Work in clean trends, poorly in ranges; best as confluence |
| Gaps | The edges of a price gap, especially the prior close | Watch them; they are not guaranteed turning points |
| Volume by price | Prices where heavy trading took place | Needs a chart of volume at each price |
| Fibonacci retracements | 38.2%, 50% and 61.8% of a swing | The most debated; use only where it overlaps a horizontal level |
| Pivot points | Formulas on the prior period's high, low and close | Objective and the same for everyone; best as confluence |

See [Indicators](learn-price-indicators.md) for moving averages and pivot points, and [Chart patterns](learn-chart-patterns.md) for trendlines in their usual shapes.

## 7. Situations and how to handle them

**A range.** Price swings between a clear ceiling and a clear floor. Draw a resistance zone across the swing highs and a support zone across the swing lows. In a wide range also mark the midpoint, where price often pauses. Each extra test weakens the edges.

**An uptrend.** Higher highs and higher lows. Broken swing highs matter most, because they tend to become support on pullbacks. Mark the most recent higher low clearly: a close below it is the first warning that the trend may be ending.

**A downtrend.** The mirror image. Broken swing lows become resistance, and a break above the most recent lower high is the first sign of a reversal.

**A breakout.** Price moves decisively through a level. Most traders want at least one of these before believing it:

- a bar **closes** beyond the zone, rather than only poking a wick through;
- the breakout bar is **larger than usual** and closes near its extreme;
- **volume rises** on the break;
- price **does not slip straight back** inside on the next few bars.

**A false breakout.** Price pushes through, triggers stops and breakout buyers, then reverses back inside. Warning signs are a long wick through the level with a close back inside, and thin volume on the break. A false breakout does not cancel the level; it often confirms that the level matters. Many traders widen the zone to include the new wick. See [Failed breakouts and bull traps](learn-strat-failed-breakouts.md).

**A retest after a breakout.** Price often returns to the broken level before going on. A successful retest touches the zone, prints a rejection bar such as a long lower wick, and moves away. A close back through the zone means the breakout failed. The trade built on this is [Pullback to support](learn-strat-pullback-support.md).

**At an all-time high.** There is no history above, so there is no resistance to draw. Use the old all-time high as support, and round numbers above as likely pauses. Do not invent resistance where none exists: strong trends at new highs can run much further than seems reasonable.

**A gap.** After a gap up, the top of the bar before the gap becomes potential support; after a gap down, its bottom becomes potential resistance. Small gaps in liquid stocks often fill within days. Large gaps on earnings may stay open for months.

**A news spike.** Results or a policy announcement can produce one or two huge bars that pierce many levels. Do not draw a level from the tip of a single spike unless price reacts there again. Where price settles after the event matters more.

**A tight consolidation.** Draw its top and bottom as a zone. After the breakout, the whole consolidation often becomes strong support or resistance, because many positions were opened there.

**Timeframes disagree.** The higher timeframe defines the zone; the lower one shows how price arrives. Slowing bars and rejection wicks on approach favour a hold. Strong, accelerating bars favour a break.

**Very old levels.** An old level can still matter, especially on weekly and monthly charts. If price has crossed it repeatedly without reacting, remove it. If price has not been there for years and it was a major turn, keep it.

## 8. Common mistakes

- **Too many levels.** If every price is near a line, remove the weakest until only a handful remain.
- **Starting on a low timeframe.** A level drawn on an hourly chart often sits just under a much stronger daily level you cannot see.
- **Treating levels as exact prices.** A stop exactly at a level sits with everyone else's. Put it beyond the whole zone, with room to spare (see [Stops and exits](learn-risk-stops-exits.md)).
- **Forcing levels to fit.** If you keep adjusting a line to touch enough bars, it is not a real level.
- **Deleting broken levels.** Keep them and relabel them.
- **Ignoring the trend.** Support in a strong downtrend is far more likely to break than the same level in an uptrend.
- **Mixing wicks and bodies.** Pick one method and apply it to every level on the chart.
- **Never updating.** Markets create new levels and wear out old ones.
- **Trading a level without confirmation.** A level says where to pay attention. Most experienced traders wait for a reaction there before acting.

## 9. What the evidence says

**Published research.** Carol Osler (2000) found that support and resistance levels published by currency firms interrupted intraday trends more often than arbitrary levels did. She later traced part of this to stop and take-profit orders clustered at round numbers (Osler, 2003). Kavajecz and Odders-White (2004) found that support and resistance levels in US stocks line up with depth in the limit-order book.

The record on trading the levels is weaker. Brock, Lakonishok and LeBaron (1992) tested a simple trading-range breakout rule on the Dow Jones index from 1897 to 1986. Buy signals were followed by higher returns than sell signals. Sullivan, Timmermann and White (1999) corrected for the many rules that could have been tried. The best rules' advantage did not survive the following decade out of sample.

**An event study on stored data.** We drew zones by the rules in sections 3 to 5, with every number fixed in advance. Then we asked what happened the next time price reached each zone.

> **Method note.** Daily prices for today's NSE and US stock lists, 2012 to October 2026. Every 126th session of every liquid stock: close at least ₹20 or $5, and 50-day average turnover at least ₹5 crore or $5 million. On that day, zones were drawn from the price history up to it: weekly first, then daily, graded, at most three each side. For each zone, we waited up to 63 sessions for price to reach its near edge. A **hold** is a close 2 ATR beyond the near edge before any close beyond the far edge, within 60 sessions of the touch. The **placebo** puts the same distances, in ATRs, under the close of a random day in the same stock and applies the same test. Caveats: today's lists only (survivorship bias), no costs, overlapping windows.

| Zone touches | Touches | Held | Placebo held |
|---|---|---|---|
| India, support | 6,634 | 39.4% | 41.3% |
| India, resistance | 8,915 | 38.9% | 37.9% |
| US, support | 25,838 | 39.9% | 40.5% |
| US, resistance | 32,516 | 33.8% | 35.1% |

| Held minus placebo | Role reversed | Not reversed | Tested five or more times |
|---|---|---|---|
| India, support | +1.6 pts | −2.4 pts | −2.7 pts |
| India, resistance | +2.5 pts | +0.8 pts | −1.7 pts |
| US, support | +1.4 pts | −0.8 pts | +0.5 pts |
| US, resistance | −0.4 pts | −1.3 pts | −3.1 pts |

What this shows:

- **The zones held no more often than chance.** The groups ranged from about two points below their placebo to one point above. A random price with the same distances did about as well.
- **Grading did not help.** Zones scoring on five or more factors fared no better against their placebo than those scoring on three or four.
- **Heavily tested levels did a little worse.** Zones already tested five or more times trailed their placebo by two to three points in three of the four groups. That fits the touches debate in section 5.
- **Role reversal was the one small plus.** Levels that had flipped from resistance to support, or the reverse, beat the placebo by one and a half to two and a half points in three of the four groups. They were a small share of all touches.
- **So use levels for planning, not prediction.** They tell you where a stop is beyond the obvious, where a target asks too much, and where to watch for a reaction. The reaction itself is the signal, not the level.

## 10. A checklist

Before you accept a level:

- I found it on the highest timeframe first, or it fits between my higher-timeframe levels.
- Price clearly turned there at least twice, or once with a very strong rejection.
- I drew it as a zone, from the body cluster to the wick cluster, and ignored a lone spike.
- It is no wider than about one typical bar's range on its timeframe.
- I checked it against round numbers, the main moving averages and the previous period's high and low.
- I graded it, and it scores on several factors.
- Another trader would probably draw something similar.

Keeping the chart current:

- **Each week:** review the weekly and daily charts. Add new major levels; remove levels price has crossed repeatedly without reacting.
- **After a breakout:** relabel the broken level with its new role rather than deleting it.
- **After a false breakout:** consider widening the zone to include the new wick.
- **Use consistent styles:** heavier lines for weekly levels, lighter for daily, so importance shows at a glance.
