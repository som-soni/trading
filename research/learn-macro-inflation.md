# Inflation — how rising prices reach rates, margins and sector leadership

**In short**

- Inflation is the rate at which prices in general rise. Central banks raise rates to fight it, and higher rates lower what stocks are worth.
- It reaches a share price three ways: lower P/Es, squeezed or widened margins, and a change in which sectors lead.
- In 2021–22 energy stocks more than doubled while technology went nowhere. In the 1970s the S&P 500 went sideways for fourteen years and lost value after inflation.
- Markets turn when inflation starts *falling*, long before it is back at target. They react to the surprise in each monthly number, not its level.
- India's inflation is driven by food, the monsoon and crude oil, much more than US inflation is.

This guide covers what inflation is and how the US and India measure it. It explains why central banks react to it and the three ways it reaches a stock price. It then follows three episodes on market data: the 1970s, the 2021–22 surge, and the slowdown in inflation that followed. Read it beside [the interest-rates guide](learn-macro-rates.md): inflation is the *cause*, rates are the *response*. [The commodities guide](learn-macro-commodities.md) covers oil and metals in their own right.

## 1. What inflation is, and why markets care

Inflation is the rate at which prices in general rise — the same basket of
goods costing more each year. A little is normal; the Fed and the RBI both aim
for a positive rate. The trouble starts when inflation is **high, rising, or
unpredictable**.

When it is, three things follow, one after another: savers demand higher
yields, the central bank raises its policy rate, and investors pay less today
for profits that arrive in the future. Each link is worth seeing on its own.

**Link 1 — savers demand higher yields.** Take a bank fixed deposit paying 7%
a year. With inflation at 4%, the saver ends the year 3% better off in what
the money can buy. That gain after inflation is the *real* return. Now let
inflation climb to 7%. The same deposit leaves the saver exactly where they
started.

Savers and bond buyers respond by refusing to lend at the old rate. They move
money to whatever pays more, and they sell existing bonds whose fixed coupons
now look poor. Selling pushes bond prices down, which pushes their yields up.
The new yield settles where lenders again expect a positive real return. When
inflation is *unpredictable*, they also ask for a little extra, as insurance
against being wrong.

**Link 2 — the central bank raises its policy rate.** The RBI and the Fed are
each charged with keeping inflation near target. Their main tool is the
short-term rate at which banks borrow. Raising it makes loans, EMIs and
credit cards dearer. Households and companies borrow and spend less, demand
cools, and sellers find it harder to raise prices.

The central bank also acts to stop a spiral. If workers expect prices to keep
rising, they ask for bigger raises, and firms pass those costs on in higher
prices. Raising rates early and visibly tells everyone that inflation will be
brought down. The effect on prices takes time, often a year or more, which is
why central banks move on where inflation is *heading*, not only where it is.

**Link 3 — investors pay less today for future profits.** A share is a claim
on profits that arrive over many years. To value it, an investor asks what each
future rupee of profit is worth today, given what the money could earn safely
in the meantime. That required return rises with yields. So the same future
profits are worth less today, even if nothing about the company has changed.

How much less depends on *when* the profits arrive. Here is ₹100 of profit,
valued at a required return of 8% and then 10%:

| Profit arrives in | Worth today at 8% | Worth today at 10% | Change |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 year | ₹92.6 | ₹90.9 | −1.8% |
| 5 years | ₹68.1 | ₹62.1 | −8.8% |
| 10 years | ₹46.3 | ₹38.6 | −16.8% |
| 20 years | ₹21.5 | ₹14.9 | −30.7% |

*Present value = 100 ÷ (1 + rate) raised to the number of years. Arithmetic, not
market data.*

A two-point rise in the required return barely touches profit due next year.
It cuts profit due in twenty years by almost a third. That is why inflation
scares hit **growth stocks** hardest: most of their value sits in profits far
in the future. A utility or a bank earning steadily today loses much less.

Higher yields also give investors a real alternative. When a government bond
or a fixed deposit pays well above inflation, a stock has to offer more to
win the money. Section 3 follows the same chain into bond markets, and
section 4 into margins and sector leadership.

**For a trader, inflation's direction and surprises matter more than its
level.** Markets already reflect the inflation path they expect. They move on
the gap between the forecast and the number actually published.

## 2. How it is measured

### United States

| Measure | What it is | Why traders care |
|---|---|---|
| **CPI** (Consumer Price Index, from the BLS, the US statistics agency) | Monthly price of a basket of consumer goods and services; housing costs are the biggest piece | The headline number; released mid-month, moves markets on the day |
| **Core CPI** | CPI excluding food and energy | Removes the parts that jump around; shows the *trend* the Fed worries about |
| **PPI** (Producer Price Index) | Prices that producers receive | Early warning — input costs that may reach consumers later |
| **PCE price index** (from the BEA, another US statistics agency) | A broader measure of what consumers spend, weighted differently | **The Fed's 2% target is defined on PCE**, and it watches core PCE most |

CPI usually runs a few tenths of a point above PCE, because housing weighs
more in CPI. Markets react to CPI, which comes out first each month. The Fed
sets policy on PCE.

### India

| Measure | What it is | Why traders care |
|---|---|---|
| **CPI (combined)** (from MoSPI, India's statistics ministry) | Consumer prices, rural + urban | **The RBI's target: 4%, with a band of 2–6%**, under the inflation-targeting framework adopted in 2016 |
| **Core CPI** | CPI excluding food and fuel (not officially published; analysts compute it) | The underlying trend once vegetables and fuel are set aside |
| **WPI** (Wholesale Price Index) | Wholesale prices, mostly manufactured goods and commodities | Shows cost pressure on manufacturers; it swings much more than CPI |

The big difference from the US: **food is close to half of India's CPI
basket**. Food and beverages were about 46% on the 2012 base. Moving the
series to a 2024 base lowers that share. But food still weighs far more than
in the US, where it is roughly a seventh.

So India's headline number is driven by the monsoon, vegetables and pulses. A
single crop (tomatoes, onions) can move it by tenths of a percent in a month.

WPI can run far above or below CPI. In 2022 WPI was in the mid-teens while CPI
was around 7%. Commodity and fuel prices hit wholesale prices first and
hardest.

## 3. Why inflation drives rates

A central bank's main lever is its policy rate. Inflation is what it is
required to control: the RBI by law, the Fed under its dual mandate (jobs and
prices). The chain:

1. Inflation runs above target → the central bank raises its policy rate, or
   signals it will.
2. Bond investors want a yield above expected inflation (a positive *real*
   yield). So **long yields rise** too, the US 10-year among them.
3. Every asset valued on its future cash flows is now discounted at a higher
   rate. Growth stocks are hit most of all.

[The interest-rates guide](learn-macro-rates.md) covers how this passes
through in detail. The order is what matters: **inflation surprises change
where rates are expected to go, and that expected path moves stocks.**

## 4. How inflation hits stocks — three channels

### 4.1 Valuations: P/Es shrink

A P/E falls when the return investors demand rises. Inflation and higher
yields raise that demanded return, even if earnings do not move. The effect
is largest for **long-duration stocks** — high-P/E growth stocks whose value
sits in profits many years away. It is the same reason a 30-year bond falls
more than a 2-year one.

### 4.2 Margins: input costs versus pricing power

Inflation is a cost to some companies and revenue to others. **Commodity
producers** sell the thing whose price is rising. **Companies with pricing
power** (strong brands, few competitors) can raise their own prices to cover
costs, after a delay.

Then there are companies **squeezed in the middle**. They buy commodities and
sell into a competitive market: paints, tyres and FMCG (everyday consumer
goods) in India; airlines and restaurants in the US. Their gross margins fall
before they can raise prices.

### 4.3 Sector leadership: who wins and who loses

| Tends to hold up or lead | Tends to lag |
|---|---|
| Energy (XLE; India: upstream oil & gas) | Long-duration growth, software, unprofitable tech (XLK, the Nasdaq 100) |
| Materials, metals, miners (XLB) | Consumer discretionary (XLY) — incomes buy less after inflation |
| Commodity-linked industrials | Rate-sensitive real estate |
| Banks, *if* rates rise without recession | Commodity *users*: paints, tyres, aviation, oil marketers in India |

This is a **tendency, not a law**. Materials held up only until April 2022,
then fell with everything else. The reliable part is energy beating
long-duration growth.

## 5. The worked example: 2021–22

US CPI inflation climbed through 2021. It peaked at about 9% in June 2022, a
four-decade high. The Fed began raising rates in March 2022, at its fastest
pace since the early 1980s.

**Note the order.** Oil peaked in March 2022, but yields kept rising for
another year and a half. That is because the Fed responds to core inflation
(services, wages, housing), which changes direction far more slowly than oil.

## 6. The 1970s — what a long inflation does

```series
title: The S&P 500 and the 10-year yield, 1965–1985
from: 1965-01-01
to: 1985-12-31
line: us:SP500_PRICE_LONG | S&P 500 (price) | log
line: us:US10Y | US 10-year yield (%) | right
mark: 1974-10-03 | 1974 low
caption: Fourteen years of going nowhere in nominal terms, while yields climbed to almost 16%.
```

The S&P 500 price index stood at about 108 at its late-1968 high. In August
1982 it was at about 102. **It went nowhere for almost fourteen years.** On
the way it fell nearly half, from January 1973 to the October 1974 low.

Dividends added a few percent a year. But consumer prices nearly tripled over
the same span. So **after inflation, stock holders lost a large part of their
purchasing power**.

The long yield climbed the whole time. The 10-year yield was about 4% in 1965.
It peaked near 15.8% in September 1981. By then the Fed, under Paul Volcker, was holding rates high enough to
break inflation. The great bull market began in August 1982, as inflation and
yields started a forty-year decline.

The lesson is not "the 1970s will repeat". It is that **a long stretch of
high, unstable inflation keeps valuations down for years**. The steady rise
an index buyer usually counts on is missing, and leadership moves to
commodities.

## 7. Disinflation and its rally (late 2022–2023)

**Disinflation** means inflation is still positive but slowing. When
inflation is high but *falling*, the market starts to bet that rate rises are
ending. The stocks that lost most while inflation rose then lead.

```series
title: The Nasdaq 100 and the 10-year as inflation came down, 2022–2023
from: 2022-08-01
to: 2023-12-31
line: us:NASDAQ100 | Nasdaq 100 (QQQ) | log
line: us:US10Y | US 10-year yield (%) | right
mark: 2022-11-10 | Soft October CPI
mark: 2023-10-19 | 10-year near 5%
caption: The soft CPI report released in November 2022 started the turn; the late-2023 fall in yields drove the year-end rally.
```

The leadership of 2022 reversed in full:

| | Period | Change |
|---|---|---|
| Nasdaq 100 (QQQ) | early October 2022 to end of 2023 | about +50% |
| S&P 500, total return | early October 2022 to end of 2023 | about +32% |
| Technology (XLK) | 2023 | about +56% |
| Energy (XLE) | 2023 | flat |

Yields did not fall straight away. The 10-year rose to almost 5% in October 2023, then
dropped to about 3.9% by year end. That final drop fuelled a sharp
November–December rally.

**The bottom came while inflation was still far above target.** Markets trade
on "is it getting better?" long before the level is comfortable.

## 8. CPI day — the inflation surprise

US CPI is released at 8:30 a.m. New York time, before the open. The market
reacts to the gap between the published number and the economists' average
forecast (the consensus) — especially for core CPI.

```series
title: Two CPI days in autumn 2022 (S&P 500 ETF)
from: 2022-08-01
to: 2022-11-30
line: us:SPY | S&P 500 (SPY)
line: us:US10Y | US 10-year yield (%) | right
mark: 2022-09-13 | Hot August CPI
mark: 2022-11-10 | Soft October CPI
caption: A core number hotter than expected knocked about 4% off the index in a day; a softer one two months later added about 5½%.
```

- **13 September 2022:** August CPI came in hotter than expected, with core
  inflation speeding up again. **SPY fell about 4.3%** (about 389 to 372), the
  Nasdaq 100 about 5.5%.
- **10 November 2022:** October CPI came in softer than expected. **SPY rose
  about 5.5%**, the Nasdaq 100 about 7.4%, and the 10-year fell about 0.3
  points in a day.

CPI day is a **scheduled volatility event**. A breakout bought the day before
is a coin flip on the number. And the price can open beyond a tight stop, so
the stop fills below it ([the macro calendar guide](learn-macro-calendar.md)).
India's CPI comes out around the 12th, after the close, so the reaction comes
at the next open. It shows mostly in rate-sensitive banks, NBFCs and real
estate.

## 9. India: food, monsoon, fuel and Brent

- **Food and the monsoon.** A weak or uneven monsoon cuts the kharif harvest
  (the crops sown in the monsoon) and lifts food prices months later. The
  IMD's forecasts (the India Meteorological Department) and the progress of
  the rains (June–September) are inflation news.
- **Vegetable spikes.** Tomato and onion prices can multiply within weeks
  after a crop failure, as tomatoes did in mid-2023. The government responds
  with export bans, limits on how much traders can stockpile, and subsidised
  sales.
- **Fuel taxes.** Pump prices are set largely by taxes: central excise duty
  and state VAT. Excise was cut in late 2021 and May 2022. The oil
  marketing companies also held pump prices steady for long stretches. So
  expensive crude often shows up in **their margins** rather than in CPI.
- **Brent and the rupee.** Dearer Brent widens the trade deficit and weakens
  the rupee. A weaker rupee makes every import dearer, including oil — a loop
  ([the currencies guide](learn-macro-currencies.md)).


| | Move |

India's CPI ran above the RBI's 6% upper band for much of 2022. The RBI
raised its repo rate from May 2022, after the Fed had begun.

Sensitive to Brent in India: oil marketing companies (hurt when pump prices
are held), and paints, tyres and aviation (their costs depend on crude).
Upstream producers, which pump the oil, gain.

## 10. What a trader does with it

1. **Know the current state.** Rising core inflation with rising yields
   punishes high-P/E growth stocks; falling core inflation rewards them.
2. **Mark CPI days.** Avoid new breakouts the day before a US CPI release.
   Trade smaller or tighten stops in rate-sensitive growth stocks (for India
   CPI, in financials).
3. **Read sector leadership.** While inflation is high, energy and materials
   groups rise in relative strength. Buy those leaders rather than fighting
   them by buying "cheap" tech ([sector rotation](learn-strat-sector-rotation.md)).
4. **Watch oil and the 10-year.** A sharp jump in either, on a day of weak
   breadth, is a reason to slow down. Periods of high inflation are volatile:
   hold smaller and fewer positions.
5. **India:** a weak monsoon plus rising Brent and a falling rupee is the mix
   that pushes the RBI towards hikes and pulls foreign money out.

## 11. What the evidence says

- **Robust:** high and rising inflation has come with lower valuations (the
  P/E falls) — in the 1970s and in 2022 alike. The mechanism is plain
  arithmetic.
- **Robust:** commodity producers outperform when commodities are what is
  driving inflation. In 2022 the gap between energy and technology was
  enormous.
- **Weaker:** "stocks are an inflation hedge". Over decades, earnings keep up
  with nominal growth. But over inflationary stretches lasting years, stocks
  have lost after inflation (the 1970s).
- **Weaker:** the sector map. Materials fell hard in late 2022. Staples and
  utilities held up because their earnings are steady, not because of
  inflation.
- **Lags:** headline inflation peaks when commodities peak. Core inflation
  and policy rates follow months later. Stocks usually bottom before
  inflation falls far (October 2022 in the US). Waiting for "inflation to be
  beaten" is usually waiting too long.
- **India:** the food-heavy CPI jumps around a lot. The RBI is more willing
  to ignore one-off vegetable spikes than persistent core inflation.

## 12. Checklist

- [ ] Last US CPI and core CPI: rising or falling on a 3- and 6-month basis?
- [ ] Next US CPI date — which open positions are exposed to it?
- [ ] What did India's last CPI show, and is it inside the RBI's 2–6% band?
- [ ] WTI and Brent: trending, spiking or calm?
- [ ] US 10-year: rising or falling over the past month?
- [ ] Are energy and materials groups rising in relative strength, and is
      technology falling?
- [ ] India: monsoon progress, vegetable prices, USD/INR trend
